Artifact Lab

Interactive tools you can try

Small working tools that connect mathematics to real decisions: stock control, forecasting, supplier choice, graph theory, personal finance and break-even analysis. Each one states its model and its limits. Everything runs in your browser, with no data sent anywhere.

EOQ and reorder point

How much should a shop order at a time, and when? This links directly to my demand prediction and replenishment project.

Inputs

Ordering costHolding costTotal cost

Model: Q* = √(2DS/H). Total cost = (D/Q)·S + (Q/2)·H, and the two parts are equal at Q*. Reorder point = daily demand × lead time + safety stock. Limits: assumes steady demand, a fixed lead time, no discounts and no stockouts. The purchase price of the goods is not included.

Demand forecasting

Paste a series of past demand values and compare two classic methods: a moving average and exponential smoothing.

Inputs

ActualMoving averageExponential smoothing

Model: the moving average forecasts the mean of the last k values. Exponential smoothing updates a level: Lt = α·xt + (1−α)·Lt−1. The error shown is the mean absolute error of one-step-ahead forecasts, measured on the same periods for both methods. Limits: neither method models trend or seasonality, so both lag a rising series.

Supplier ranking

Choosing a supplier is a multi-criteria decision. Edit the numbers and the weights to see how the ranking changes.

Importance of each criterion

Model: a weighted sum. Price and lead time (lower is better) are scored as best ÷ value. Quality and delivery reliability (higher is better) are scored as value ÷ best. Each score is multiplied by its weight and the total is shown out of 100. Limits: the result depends on the weights you choose, so it supports a decision and does not replace one.

Metric dimension of the Petersen graph

Metric dimension is a graph theory idea: pick a few "landmark" vertices so that every vertex has a different list of distances to them. Click vertices to choose landmarks and see whether they work.

Select a vertex to begin.

Idea: the distance vector of vertex v is (d(v,L1), d(v,L2), …). If two vertices share a vector, the landmarks cannot tell them apart. The smallest number of landmarks that works is the metric dimension, and for the Petersen graph it is 3. Limits: this demo covers one fixed graph with 10 vertices.

Loan and savings calculators

Two everyday money questions: what will a loan cost me each month, and how much will regular savings grow to?

Loan instalment

Model: a reducing-balance loan with equal monthly instalments, EMI = P·r·(1+r)n / ((1+r)n − 1), where r is the monthly rate. Limits: fixed rate, no fees or insurance. Real lenders may calculate differently, so use this to understand the idea and check the offer you are given.

Savings growth

Balance with returnsMoney you put in

Model: monthly compounding with deposits at the end of each month, FV = A·(1+r)n + M·((1+r)n − 1)/r. Limits: assumes a constant return, and ignores inflation, tax and fees. Real returns vary from year to year.

Break-even analysis

How many items must a small business sell before it stops losing money? This is a basic decision tool for any product or shop.

Inputs

Sales incomeTotal cost

Model: break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost). Profit = (price − variable cost) × units − fixed costs. Limits: assumes one product, a constant price and constant cost per unit, which is rarely exactly true at large volumes.
